The big idea

  • The edge from \(A \to Y\) is the causal effect we want to measure
  • The edge from \(X \to Y\) is what we’re correcting for when we do covariate adjustment
  • The edge from \(X \to A\) is what we’re correcting for when we do balancing
  • What if we simultaneously do both? How can we do that?

(Bang and Robins 2005)

Double robustness

The core insight: An estimator is doubly robust if it remains consistent when either (but not necessarily both):

  1. Outcome regression model is correctly specified, or
  2. Treatment assignment model (propensity score) is correctly specified

Why this matters:

  • Model misspecification is inevitable in practice
  • We get “two chances” to be right instead of one
  • Combines the best features of:
    • G-computation: models the outcome regression \(\mu(X) = E[Y \mid A=1, X]\)
    • Inverse probability weighting: models the propensity score \(e(X) = P(A=1 \mid X)\) and reweights

Key theoretical result: Under standard assumptions, DR estimators achieve the semiparametric efficiency bound when both models are correct.

Setup and notation

  • Data: \((A_i, X_i, Y_i)_{i=1}^n\), with \(A_i \in \{0,1\}\) a sampling indicator: only see \(Y_i\) when \(A_i = 1\)
  • Target: \(\psi = E[Y]\)
  • Identification: MAR, \(Y \perp A \mid X\)

Two nuisance functions, in last week’s notation, each with a parametric working model:

Truth Working model
Propensity score \(e(X) = P(A = 1 \mid X)\) \(e_\alpha(X)\)
Outcome regression \(\mu(X) = E[Y \mid A = 1, X]\) \(\mu_{\beta,\phi}(X)\)

Bang & Robins write \(\pi(X;\alpha)\), \(e(X;\beta,\phi)\) and \(\mu\). We write \(e_\alpha(X)\), \(\mu_{\beta,\phi}(X)\) and \(\psi\).

The DR estimator

Bang & Robins, linear case. Three steps:

  1. Fit the propensity model: \(\hat{e}(X) = e_{\hat{\alpha}}(X)\)
  1. Fit the outcome model with \(\hat{e}^{-1}\) as an extra regressor, by OLS among the observed (\(A_i = 1\)): \[\mu_{\beta,\phi}(X_i) = X_i^\top \beta + \phi\, \hat{e}(X_i)^{-1}, \qquad 0 = \sum_i A_i \begin{pmatrix} X_i \\ \hat{e}(X_i)^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \big(Y_i - \mu_{\beta,\phi}(X_i)\big)\] Call the fit \(\hat{\mu} = \mu_{\hat{\beta},\hat{\phi}}\).
  1. Average the fitted values over everyone, observed or not: \[\hat{\psi}_{dr} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \hat{\mu}(X_i)\]

Step 3 is just averaging over the model. All we did was add \(\hat{e}^{-1}\) in step 2.

Why is it DR? Step 0: rewrite the estimator

Shorthand: \(\hat{e}_i = \hat{e}(X_i)\) and \(\hat{\mu}_i = \hat{\mu}(X_i)\).

Truth: \(e(X) = P(A=1 \mid X)\), \(\mu(X) = E[Y \mid A=1, X]\), and \(\psi = E[Y] = E[\mu(X)]\).

  • \(\hat{e}_i^{-1}\) is a regressor in the outcome fit, its normal equation holds in sample: \[\sum_i A_i\, \hat{e}_i^{-1}\, (Y_i - \hat{\mu}_i) = 0\]
  • Add this zero to \(\hat{\psi}_{dr}\) and it becomes a sum of two familiar pieces: \[\hat{\psi}_{dr} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \hat{\mu}_i}_{\text{G-computation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \frac{A_i}{\hat{e}_i}\,(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_i)}_{\text{IPW, applied to residuals}}\]
  • This is the augmented IPW form. Plan: hold one model at its truth, let the other be anything, and take expectations term by term using MAR (\(Y \perp A \mid X\)) and iterated expectations.

Why is it DR? Step 1: the outcome model is right

Suppose \(\hat{\mu}_i \to \mu(X_i)\). Make no assumption about \(\hat{e}\) beyond \(\hat{e} > 0\).

First term. MAR lets us drop the conditioning on \(A = 1\): \[E[\mu(X)] = E\big[E[Y \mid A=1, X]\big] = E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = E[Y] = \psi\]

Second term. Condition on \(X\); \(\hat{e}(X)\) is then a constant and comes outside: \[E\left[\frac{A}{\hat{e}(X)}\,(Y - \mu(X))\right] = E\left[\frac{1}{\hat{e}(X)}\; E\big[A\,(Y - \mu(X)) \mid X\big]\right]\] and the inner expectation is zero because \(A(Y - \mu)\) is only nonzero when \(A = 1\), where \(\mu\) is the conditional mean: \[E\big[A\,(Y - \mu(X)) \mid X\big] = e(X)\; E\big[Y - \mu(X) \mid A=1, X\big] = e(X) \cdot 0 = 0\]

  • The correction term vanishes for any \(\hat{e}\): a wrong propensity model just reweights residuals that are already mean-zero given \(X\). So \(\hat{\psi}_{dr} \to \psi\); the correction only adds noise.

Why is it DR? Step 2: the propensity score is right

Suppose \(\hat{e}_i \to e(X_i)\), and let \(\hat{\mu}_i \to \mu^*(X_i)\) for an arbitrary (wrong) \(\mu^*\).

Regroup the two pieces by what multiplies \(\mu^*\): \[\hat{\psi}_{dr} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \frac{A_i\, Y_i}{e(X_i)} \;+\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left(1 - \frac{A_i}{e(X_i)}\right) \mu^*(X_i)\]

First term. Horvitz–Thompson (last week). MAR splits \(E[AY \mid X]\) into a product: \[E\left[\frac{A\,Y}{e(X)}\right] = E\left[\frac{E[A \mid X]\; E[Y \mid X]}{e(X)}\right] = E\big[E[Y \mid X]\big] = \psi\]

Second term. Condition on \(X\); only \(A\) is random inside: \[E\left[\left(1 - \frac{A}{e(X)}\right) \mu^*(X)\right] = E\left[\left(1 - \frac{E[A \mid X]}{e(X)}\right) \mu^*(X)\right] = E\big[0 \cdot \mu^*(X)\big] = 0\]

  • The outcome model enters only through a term with mean exactly zero: it cannot bias, only change variance. Steps 1 and 2 are mirror images: when one model is right, the other’s term is inert.

Why is it DR? Both at once: the bias is a product

Let both limits be wrong: \(\hat{\mu} \to \mu^*\) and \(\hat{e} \to e^*\). Taking expectations of the augmented form, with \(\psi = E[\mu(X)]\): \[E[\hat{\psi}_{dr}] - \psi = E[\mu^*(X)] + E\left[\frac{e(X)}{e^*(X)}\,\big(\mu(X) - \mu^*(X)\big)\right] - E[\mu(X)]\]

Collect the \(\mu - \mu^*\) terms: \[E[\hat{\psi}_{dr}] - \psi = E\left[\big(\mu(X) - \mu^*(X)\big)\;\frac{e(X) - e^*(X)}{e^*(X)}\right]\]

  • Either factor zero, bias zero. Steps 1 and 2 in a single line.
  • Both a little wrong: the bias is a product of two errors, which is smaller than either. This is the seed of double ML: rates multiply.
  • Both moderately wrong, with small \(e^*\): the product is not small, and the denominator makes it worse. This is Kang & Schafer’s cautionary tale.

OLS is DR? Step 1: Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

Regress \(Y\) on \(A\) and \(X\) (intercept in \(X\)); call the coefficient on \(A\) \(\hat{\tau}_{OLS}\). Partial regression purges \(X\) from both variables first: \(\tilde{A}_i = A_i - X_i^\top \hat{\gamma}\) and \(\tilde{Y}_i = Y_i - X_i^\top \hat{\delta}\).

FWL: the multiple-regression coefficient is the regression of residual on residual, \[\hat{\tau}_{OLS} = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{A}_i \tilde{Y}_i}{\sum_i \tilde{A}_i^2}\] an exact in-sample identity.

Two readings of the same formula:

  • \(\hat{\tau}_{OLS} = \sum_i w_i \tilde{Y}_i\) with \(w_i = \tilde{A}_i / \sum_j \tilde{A}_j^2\): a weighting estimator, and \(\sum_i w_i X_i = 0\) is exact mean balance on \(X\) (last week’s constraint with \(f(X) = X\)).
  • \(X_i^\top \hat{\gamma}\) is a fitted linear probability model for \(P(A_i = 1 \mid X_i)\), so \(\tilde{A}_i = A_i - \hat{e}^{\text{lin}}(X_i)\). OLS carries a propensity model inside it: linear in \(X\), fit by least squares.

OLS is DR? Step 2: two models inside one regression

One regression, two working models: outcome \(E[Y \mid A, X] = \tau A + X^\top \beta\) and implicit propensity \(E[A \mid X] = X^\top \gamma\).

Route 1: outcome model right. \(Y_i = \tau A_i + X_i^\top\beta + \varepsilon_i\) with \(E[\varepsilon_i \mid A_i, X_i] = 0\), so \(\hat{\tau}_{OLS} \to \tau\). Textbook.

Route 2: propensity model right, outcome model wrong. Let \(Y_i = \tau A_i + g(X_i) + \varepsilon_i\) with \(g\) arbitrary. Substitute into FWL, using \(\sum_i \tilde{A}_i A_i = \sum_i \tilde{A}_i^2\): \[\hat{\tau}_{OLS} - \tau = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{A}_i\, g(X_i) + \sum_i \tilde{A}_i\, \varepsilon_i}{\sum_i \tilde{A}_i^2}\] If \(E[A \mid X] = X^\top\gamma\), then in the population \(\tilde{A} = A - X^\top\gamma\) has \(E[\tilde{A} \mid X] = 0\), so by iterated expectations \(E[\tilde{A}\, g(X)] = E\big[E[\tilde{A} \mid X]\, g(X)\big] = 0\) for every \(g\).

  • The omitted nonlinearity is orthogonal to the residualised treatment, so \(\hat{\tau}_{OLS} \to \tau\) with \(E[Y \mid A, X]\) misspecified. Same structure as Bang & Robins Step 2.

OLS is DR? Step 3: the heteroskedasticity catch

Route 2 assumed a constant \(\tau\). Let effects vary, \(\tau(X) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X]\), with only the propensity model right, so \(\tilde{A} = A - e(X)\). Conditional on \(X\): \[E[\tilde{A}\, Y \mid X] = e(X)\big(1 - e(X)\big)\, \tau(X), \qquad E[\tilde{A}^2 \mid X] = e(X)\big(1 - e(X)\big)\]

\[\hat{\tau}_{OLS} \;\to\; \frac{E[\tilde{A}\, Y]}{E[\tilde{A}^2]} = \frac{E\big[e(X)\,(1 - e(X))\; \tau(X)\big]}{E\big[e(X)\,(1 - e(X))\big]}\]

  • This is (Aronow and Samii 2016): a conditional-variance-weighted effect.
    • Units with \(e(X)\) near \(1/2\) count most.
    • The heteroskedasticity is in the treatment: \(\text{Var}(A \mid X) = e(1-e)\).
  • It equals the ATE when \(\tau(X)\) is constant or uncorrelated with \(e(X)(1-e(X))\).
    • Otherwise OLS is doubly robust for a different estimand.

Punchline: OLS is DR for a constant effect because it balances \(X\) exactly in sample. Bang & Robins put \(\hat{e}^{-1}\) into the outcome regression on purpose; OLS puts a linear \(\hat{e}\) in by accident.

Bang & Robins simulation results

Setup: Multiple data-generating processes with different model specifications

  1. When both models correct:
    • Low bias, good coverage
    • Achieves efficiency gains over single-model approaches
  2. When only outcome model correct:
    • \(\hat{\mu}_{DR}\) remains consistent
    • Outperforms pure IPW approaches (\(\hat{\mu}_{HT}\))
  3. When only propensity score correct:
    • \(\hat{\mu}_{DR}\) remains consistent
    • Outperforms pure outcome regression (\(\hat{\mu}_{OR}\))
  4. When both models wrong: All methods can fail.

The “double protection”: DR gives you robustness against misspecification of either model, dramatically improving practical performance.

What Bang & Robins opened up

Insight: We don’t need to get one model exactly right. We can hedge our bets.

Key innovations:

  • Unified framework connecting outcome regression and IPW
  • Formal double robustness theory
  • Practical estimators that “just work” in many settings

Issues:

  • Why should we expect to get even one model right?
    • Yes, two lottery tickets give you a better chance than one.
  • How should we do all of this estimation flexibly?
    • Replace parametric models with ML, IPW with balancing weights?

Open questions

  1. Machine learning integration: What happens when we soup-up Frisch-Waugh-Lovell with machine learning?
    • Motivates: (Chernozhukov et al. 2018) on double/debiased ML
  2. Optimal bias-variance tradeoffs: Can we do better than simple plug-in estimation? How do we target the parameter of interest more directly?
    • Motivates: (Schuler and Rose 2017) on targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), with more detail in (Van der Laan, Rose, et al. 2011).
  3. When do DR methods actually work? Are there settings where all approaches fail catastrophically?
    • Motivates: (Kang and Schafer 2007) empirical comparison studies
  4. Modern theoretical foundations: How do we unify these ideas with modern semiparametric theory and machine learning?
    • Motivates: (Kennedy 2022) comprehensive review

The promise and peril of causal inference

So far this semester: We’ve assumed strong identification conditions

  • Randomization (experiments)
  • Unconfoundedness (selection on observables)
  • Parallel trends (difference-in-differences)

These assumptions allow us to point identify causal effects

But what happens when these assumptions fail?

  • Unobserved confounding
  • Lack of overlap in covariate distributions
  • Missing outcome data
  • Violations of SUTVA

The traditional choice: Make untestable assumptions or give up entirely

Is there a middle ground?

The partial identification alternative

Key insight from Manski (1990): Even without point identification, data often restrict the range of possible treatment effects

The approach:

  • Bound parameters rather than estimate them precisely
  • Be honest about uncertainty while still being informative
  • Ask: “What can we learn with minimal assumptions?”

Manski’s “worst-case bounds”: What can we say about treatment effects using only:

  • The observed data
  • The support of the outcome variable
  • No other assumptions

The answer: Surprisingly much! The bounds may be wide, but they’re assumption-free.

(Manski 1990)

Setup: The missing data problem

The estimand: Average treatment effect

\[\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]\]

The fundamental problem of causal inference: We never observe both \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) for the same unit

What we observe:

\[Y_i = A_i Y_i(1) + (1 - A_i) Y_i(0)\]

where \(A_i = 1\) if unit \(i\) receives treatment, 0 otherwise

This creates missing data on potential outcomes

  • For treated units: \(Y_i(0)\) is missing
  • For control units: \(Y_i(1)\) is missing

Point identification requires filling in the missing data using assumptions (randomization, unconfoundedness, etc.)

No-assumptions bounds: The logic

Question: What can we say about \(\tau\) without any assumptions (or data)?

Key observation: For each unit, the treatment effect \(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) must lie in some range

If the outcome has support \(Y \in [y_L, y_U]\), then:

\[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \in [y_L - y_U, y_U - y_L]\]

Example: If \(Y\) is binary (0 or 1):

  • Worst case for treated: \(Y_i(1) = 0\) and \(Y_i(0) = 1 \implies\) effect = \(-1\)
  • Best case for treated: \(Y_i(1) = 1\) and \(Y_i(0) = 0 \implies\) effect = \(+1\)

Averaging over the population gives bounds on \(\tau\)

No-assumptions bounds: The math

For the treated group:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1] &= \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1]\\ &= \text{observed} - \text{unobserved} \end{aligned}\]

Bound the unobserved quantity: \(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1] \in [y_L, y_U]\)

This gives bounds on ATT:

\[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=1] - y_U \leq \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1] \leq \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=1] - y_L\]

Similarly for the control group:

\[y_L - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=0] \leq \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=0] \leq y_U - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=0]\]

Bounds on ATE: Weighted average of bounds on ATT and ATC

Width of bounds: Depends directly on the range of \(Y\), i.e., \(y_U - y_L\)

Tightening bounds with assumptions

The tradeoff: Bounds with no assumptions may be wide and uninformative

Solution: Add assumptions to tighten bounds

Monotone Treatment Response (MTR): Treatment weakly increases (or decreases) outcomes for everyone

\[Y_i(1) \geq Y_i(0) \quad \text{for all } i\]

How MTR tightens bounds on ATT:

  • Without MTR: \(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1] \in [y_L, y_U]\)
  • With MTR: \(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1] \in [y_L, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=1]]\)
  • The upper bound shrinks from \(y_U\) to the observed mean!

General principle: bounds = what we observe + assumptions about what we don’t observe

The key question: Are the additional assumptions credible?

Connection to selection

Observation: The width of bounds reflects the strength of selection into treatment

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1] = {} & \mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=1] -\mathbb{E}[Y \mid D=0]\\ & + \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=0]- \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1] \end{aligned}\]

Selection bias: \(\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=0]- \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid D=1]\)

What this means: Bounds express the possibility of selection bias

Why bounds matter

A fundamental tension in causal inference:

  • Precise but wrong: Point estimates with invalid assumptions
  • Honest but wide: Bounds with minimal assumptions

Which is better?

Better to report \([−0.5, 0.8]\) honestly than \(0.15\) (s.e. \(0.08\)) with false precision

Bounds enable falsification without strong assumptions:

  • If the null effect (zero) lies outside the bounds, we can reject it
  • If bounds include zero, we learn that no-assumptions approach cannot reject the null
  • This is informative! It tells us we need stronger assumptions to draw conclusions

Policy implications:

  • Bounds can rule out effects below a certain threshold
  • Example: If lower bound on wage effect is \(\$2\)/hour, we know effect is at least that large

Sharp bounds and extensions

Definition: Bounds are “sharp” if they are the tightest possible given the assumptions

Manski’s no-assumptions bounds are sharp: You cannot do better without adding assumptions

The modern literature builds on this foundation in several ways:

  1. Additional shape restrictions: Monotonicity, convexity, smoothness
  2. Parametric/semiparametric models: Impose structure on potential outcomes
  3. Additional data sources: Instrumental variables, proxy variables, negative controls
  4. Machine learning: Estimate bounds flexibly without parametric assumptions

Key principle: Every tightening requires a justifiable assumption

Your classmates’ presentations: Show how modern methods tighten bounds while maintaining credibility

(Aronow, Green, and Lee 2014)

The variance is partially identified

Week 1: under complete randomization,

\[\text{Var}_A[\hat{\tau} \mid \mathcal{S}] = \frac{s_{Y(1)}^2}{N_1} + \frac{s_{Y(0)}^2}{N_0} - \frac{s_{Y(1)}^2 + s_{Y(0)}^2 - 2\hlred{s_{Y(1),Y(0)}}}{N}\]

  • Randomization identifies each marginal: the treated are a random sample of the \(Y_i(1)\), the controls of the \(Y_i(0)\)
  • The covariance needs the joint: which \(Y_i(0)\) goes with which \(Y_i(1)\)
  • Manski’s missing-data problem again: bound the unidentified piece by what the marginals allow

Two upper bounds on the variance:

  • Neyman: \(s_\tau^2 \geq 0\), i.e. \(s_{Y(1),Y(0)} \leq (s_{Y(1)}^2 + s_{Y(0)}^2)/2\)
  • Cauchy–Schwarz: \(s_{Y(1),Y(0)} \leq s_{Y(1)} s_{Y(0)}\), so \(s_\tau^2 \geq (s_{Y(1)} - s_{Y(0)})^2\)
  • Neither is sharp: correlation \(1\) is only attainable if the marginals are the same up to an increasing affine map

The rearrangement inequality

For \(a_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq a_{(N)}\), \(b_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq b_{(N)}\) and any permutation \(\sigma\):

\[\sum_i a_{(i)} b_{(N+1-i)} \;\leq\; \sum_i a_{(i)} b_{\sigma(i)} \;\leq\; \sum_i a_{(i)} b_{(i)}\]

The covariance is largest when sorted together, smallest when sorted against each other. Same marginals, three pairings:

Sharp bounds on the variance

In the population, sorting means pairing quantiles by rank. With \(F_a\) the distribution of \(Y_i(a)\):

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma^H &= \textstyle\int_0^1 F_1^{-1}(u)\,F_0^{-1}(u)\,du - \mu_1\mu_0\\ \sigma^L &= \textstyle\int_0^1 F_1^{-1}(u)\,F_0^{-1}(1-u)\,du - \mu_1\mu_0 \end{aligned}\]

  • Substituting these for the covariance gives \([V^L, V^H]\), the sharp identified set
  • Estimation: each arm is a random sample from \(F_a\), so plug in its empirical quantiles
  • Neyman \(\geq\) Cauchy–Schwarz \(\geq V^H \geq\) true variance

How much tighter?

\(Y_i(0)\) right-skewed, \(Y_i(1)\) bimodal, \(N_1 = N_0 = 100\); the truth is the random pairing

  • Cauchy–Schwarz barely improves on Neyman: \(s_{Y(1)} \approx s_{Y(0)}\)
  • The sharp upper bound is 13% smaller: the marginals’ shapes, not just their spreads, rule out perfect correlation

(Imbens and Manski 2004)

Hypothesis testing with bounds

The setup: You’ve estimated bounds \([\hat{\theta}_L, \hat{\theta}_U]\) with standard errors \(\text{se}(\hat{\theta}_L)\) and \(\text{se}(\hat{\theta}_U)\)

How to construct a \((1-\alpha)\) confidence interval?

Imbens and Manski (2004): Use one-sided tests at each endpoint!

\[\left[\hat{\theta}_L - z_{\alpha} \cdot \text{se}(\hat{\theta}_L), \quad \hat{\theta}_U + z_{\alpha} \cdot \text{se}(\hat{\theta}_U)\right]\]

Why \(z_\alpha\) instead of \(z_{\alpha/2}\)?

  • Lower bound: only care if true \(\theta_L\) is below \(\hat{\theta}_L\) (one-sided)
  • Upper bound: only care if true \(\theta_U\) is above \(\hat{\theta}_U\) (one-sided)
  • This is less overly conservative!

Refinement: Can use data-dependent critical value to improve finite-sample behavior

For hypothesis testing \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\):

  • Reject if \(\theta_0\) lies outside the confidence interval
